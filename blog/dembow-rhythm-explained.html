Every reggaetón track you have ever danced to is built on one drum pattern. It has a name: dembow. Understand it and you understand why perreo moves the way it does.

What is dembow?

Dembow is the drum pattern at the core of reggaetón: a steady kick on each beat with a syncopated snare accent that repeats in a 3+3+2 grouping. It takes its name from Shabba Ranks’s 1990 dancehall hit “Dem Bow”.

Jamaica, 1990

“Dem Bow” by Shabba Ranks used a riddim produced by Steely & Clevie. The riddim became one of the most reused in dancehall history.

Panama and Puerto Rico

Panamanian artists such as El General translated that rhythm into Spanish. In the mid-1990s Puerto Rican producers like DJ Playero built whole tracks on it, and reggaetón took shape.

The 3+3+2 pattern

Count eight steps. The accents land on steps 1, 4 and 7: three, three, two. Dancers feel it as “boom — ch — boom-chick”. The ear hears the same loop over and over, which is why the beat is so easy to move to and so hard to forget.

Tempo

Classic reggaetón usually sits around 90 to 100 BPM. Dominican dembow is much faster, often above 120 BPM. Slower tempos give room for low, controlled hip work; faster ones push dancers into quick isolations.

How dancers use it

Good dancers do not just hit the kick. They play with the off-beat, pausing on the dembow gap and then hitting the next accent. That contrast is what separates a clean perreo from a random one.

Frequently asked questions

What is dembow?
Dembow is the drum pattern behind reggaetón: a steady kick with a syncopated snare accent in a 3+3+2 grouping. The name comes from Shabba Ranks’s 1990 song “Dem Bow”.
Is dembow the same as reggaetón?
Not exactly. Dembow is the rhythm; reggaetón is the genre built on it, together with rap or singing in Spanish and other production choices. Dominican dembow is also a separate, faster genre.
What BPM is reggaetón?
Typically around 90 to 100 BPM. Dominican dembow often runs at 120 BPM or more.
Who created the dembow beat?
The riddim behind “Dem Bow” was produced by Steely & Clevie in Jamaica. Panamanian and Puerto Rican artists later adapted it into Spanish-language music.
